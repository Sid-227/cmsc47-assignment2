Write Up

Dataset

We use ProPublica’s July 2020 NYPD Civilian Complaint Review Board release. The file has 33,358 allegation records tied to 12,056 complaints and 3,996 officers. A complaint can include multiple allegations or officers, so both charts count allegation records.

Download the aggregate subset (CSV) ↓

White hat design

The first chart shows three possible results for allegations: substantiated, unsubstantiated, and exonerated. All types of substantiated recommendations are grouped together. The chart is about whether the allegation was supported by the finding, not about what penalty was recommended.

Each bar uses a fixed scale from 0% to 100%. Its position and length show the percentage of allegations in each outcome. Labels include both the percentage and the number of allegations, so readers can see the share and the count.

Definitions appear near the chart to explain the difference between unsubstantiated and exonerated. Unsubstantiated means there was not enough evidence to decide that the allegation was supported. Exonerated means the conduct was allowed under the rules.

The second chart shows the substantiation percentage for each type of misconduct. It also marks the overall percentage for the selected year, making it easier to compare each category with the yearly total. Labels show how many percentage points each category is above or below that overall rate.

The year filter changes both charts. The category filter narrows the first chart and highlights the matching row in the second chart. The overall rate is weighted by the number of allegations, so categories with more allegations count more toward the baseline.

Black hat design

The black-hat chart is designed to suggest that about three quarters of allegations cleared the officers involved. The calculation is correct for this file: there are 15,448 unsubstantiated allegation records and 9,609 exonerated records. Together, that is 25,057 of 33,358 records, or 75.1%.

The misleading part is what the chart says that number means. It combines unsubstantiated and exonerated outcomes into one group called “Cleared.” But those outcomes are different. Unsubstantiated means there was not enough evidence to support the allegation, it does not mean the allegation was false. Exonerated means the conduct was permitted.

The chart also changes the focus from allegation records to people. Its title says “officers cleared,” and its group label says “Cleared,” even though the counts are about allegations. Those words encourage readers to conclude that the officers were cleared, which the combined figure does not establish.

The chart keeps a 0–100% scale and includes a real source credit. Those choices make it look reliable. The misleading effect comes from combining different outcomes and using labels that overstate what the data shows, rather than from changing the numbers or cutting off the scale.
