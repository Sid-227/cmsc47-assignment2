Read the white-hat design rationale

This visualization asks what the CCRB concluded about the allegations included in ProPublica’s July 2020 release. The file has 33,358 allegation records associated with 12,056 complaints and 3,996 officers. A complaint can involve multiple allegations or officers, so the charts consistently count allegation records. The release is selected: it covers closed, fully investigated cases involving officers still serving in late June 2020 who had at least one substantiated allegation, and excludes unfounded allegations. It therefore cannot estimate misconduct across the entire NYPD.

The first chart separates substantiated, unsubstantiated, and exonerated outcomes. All substantiated recommendation subtypes are combined because the question concerns the finding, not the recommended response. Horizontal position and bar length encode percentages on a fixed zero-to-100 scale. Direct labels provide both percentages and counts; distinct colors help identify outcomes without requiring color alone. Nearby definitions make the crucial difference between insufficient evidence and permitted conduct explicit.

The second chart aggregates allegations by misconduct category and calculates each category’s substantiation percentage. A reference line marks the overall percentage for the selected received year, and labels report percentage-point differences from that baseline. Counts make the different group sizes visible. The year filter affects both charts; the category filter narrows the first chart and highlights its corresponding comparison row, preserving the other categories as context. The baseline is weighted by allegation counts, rather than averaging category percentages.

Received year describes when a complaint was received, not when an incident happened or when a case closed. The final year is incomplete. Repeated allegations involving the same complaint or officer are not independent observations, so no population-level confidence intervals or causal claims are offered. All transformations are reproducible in the preparation script, and the aggregate download allows readers to check the calculations.

02 / BLACK HAT

When uncertainty becomes reassurance.

An intentionally misleading presentation for this assignment. Read the chart first, then reveal its construction.

COMPLAINT REVIEW / HISTORICAL OUTCOMES

Three in four allegations leave officers cleared

A broad view of the investigation record, 1985–2020.

75.1%of allegations cleared

Source: ProPublica / CCRB, July 2020 release · 33,358 allegation records. This panel always uses the full release.

The arithmetic was right. The category was wrong. The reassuring 75.1% combines 46.3% unsubstantiated allegations with 28.8% exonerated allegations. Only the latter records a finding that the conduct was permitted. The remaining 24.9% were substantiated. No records were added or removed by this toggle.
Read the black-hat design rationale

The black-hat panel promotes an unsupported reading: that roughly three quarters of allegations cleared the officers involved. Its arithmetic is accurate for this file, which makes the framing harder to detect. There are 15,448 unsubstantiated and 9,609 exonerated allegation records. Adding them produces 25,057, or 75.1% of the 33,358 records. The deceptive step is interpreting that combined total as vindication.

The first manipulation is data transformation. Two substantively different outcomes are merged into one reassuring group. An exonerated finding means the alleged conduct happened but was permitted under NYPD rules. An unsubstantiated finding means the investigation did not establish the alleged misconduct; it does not establish innocence or that the allegation was false. Collapsing these categories erases uncertainty and lets the larger unresolved group borrow the meaning of the smaller exonerated group.

The second manipulation is titles and labels. “Officers cleared” encourages a conclusion about people even though the denominator consists of allegations, and the label “Cleared” strengthens that inference. The broad historical subtitle also gives little prominence to the release’s selection criteria. These officers are not a representative sample of all NYPD officers, and unfounded allegations are not included. A general claim about police behavior cannot follow from this selected collection.

The design uses a large percentage, calm green emphasis, a restrained layout, and a genuine source credit to resemble a credible report. The scale remains zero to 100; the deception relies on meaning rather than an obvious geometric distortion. The reveal button restores the three original outcome groups and replaces the headline with an accurate statement, while holding the underlying records and denominator constant. This comparison demonstrates how a chart can preserve every number yet change the story. The panel is explicitly identified as an educational black-hat example so its unsupported claim is not presented as this project’s conclusion.

03 / BEHIND THE CHARTS

A record of the decisions.

Data & reproducibility

The original ZIP is CCRB-Complaint-Data_202007271729.zip. All CSV rows are retained; substantiated recommendation subtypes are grouped. The public CSV contains only year × category × outcome counts, with no names or identifiers.

Percent = group count ÷ relevant allegation count × 100. Percentage-point difference = category substantiation percentage − overall percentage for the same received-year selection. Values are rounded only for display.

Development process

The student selected the dataset, reviewed the concept and rubric, and requested the course labs’ coding style. Codex inspected the data, implemented the D3 charts, drafted the explanations, and performed verification. Work proceeded through documentation review, aggregation, visual implementation, and interaction checks.

AI usage & reflection

Codex assisted with design exploration, data inspection, coding, and writing. Rubric review prompted a category comparison with percentage-point calculations and a reveal interaction that isolates the effect of grouping. The student directed the use of familiar lab patterns rather than a framework.

AI-generated framing can sound authoritative while overstating what data supports. Here, checking the README changed the scope statement: this release is a selected group of officers, not the entire NYPD. Human review of the final explanations remains part of submission preparation.
